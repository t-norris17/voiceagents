INTRUST 401(k) · Robin · Art of the possible

Three things
Robin can do

Three phone calls. Each one takes about three minutes and shows a different thing this technology can do. Pick up the phone, be the person on the card, and ask the questions in order.

How to run one

  1. Dial Robin and put her on speaker.
  2. When she asks who is calling, read the Member ID and date of birth from the card. She will never ask for a Social Security number.
  3. Ask the questions in order. The grey line under each one is what to listen for.
  4. The red question on each page is the moment worth pointing out.
1 Marcus, 28 A new employee getting set up. She handles the whole onboarding conversation.
2 Priya, 52 Mid-career, asking for advice. She answers what she can and refuses what she can't.
3 Dana, 73 About to retire. The highest-stakes call, and the one where she hands off.
Before you dial

All three people are invented, and so is every dollar figure. No real member has ever been through this system. Robin answers only from INTRUST's own plan documents — if something isn't in them, she says so rather than guessing.

She can onboard
a new employee

Marcus is twenty-eight and two years in. He has never looked at his 401(k) and has four questions. This is the call a human would spend fifteen minutes on.

Open with

"Hi — I just started looking at my 401(k) and I've got a few questions."

Then ask, in order
1
"Was I automatically enrolled? At what rate?"
Six percent, before tax, rising a little each year, into the plan's default fund. Her strongest answer — straight out of INTRUST's own notice.
2
"How do I change how much I'm putting in?"
She walks him through the real screens on the NestEgg U site, or offers the phone number. Not a description of a process — the actual steps.
3
"Can I roll my old 401(k) from my last job into this one?"
Yes, plus who to call to start it. Note that she offers the next step rather than stopping at "yes".
4
"What's my balance?" — then: "why are those two numbers different?"
The moment. She reads $27,318.15, then $19,122.70 vested, and explains the gap: employer money that isn't fully his yet. She knows the difference between what he has and what he owns — and she read both from his record rather than estimating.
What this shows

One call replaced an entire onboarding conversation — four unrelated questions, answered from plan documents and from his own account, without a person picking up.

Open with

"Hi, I wanted to check on a couple of things with my retirement account."

Then ask, in order
1
"I'm over fifty — can I put in extra?"
Yes: catch-up contributions. Worth noticing that this answer only matters because of her age, and she gives it without being told how old she is.
2
"Do those extra contributions get matched too?"
A real follow-up question, not a restart. She keeps the thread.
3
"What's my balance?"
$214,905.62, fully vested. Read from her record.
4
"Which fund should I be in?" — then push: "come on, just tell me what you'd pick."
The moment. She will not answer, no matter how you press. She explains that she can't give investment advice and hands over to INTRUST's Participant Investment Advice line. This is built into her instructions, not left to her judgement.
What this shows

The boundary holds under pressure. She is a plan-information agent, not an adviser, and the line between the two is enforced rather than hoped for.

Open with

"Hi — I'm getting close to retiring and I want to understand my options."

Then ask, in order
1
"What's my balance?"
$487,213.44, fully vested. Half a million dollars, read back correctly, to someone she has verified.
2
"When can I start taking money out?"
Leaving the company, disability, or death — plus the plan's other options. A genuinely complicated answer, given plainly.
3
"What would happen to it if I got divorced?"
Answered. An uncomfortable question a member might not want to ask a person.
4
"I turn seventy-three this year — when do I have to start taking money out?"
The moment. Say "watch what she does here" before you ask it. Robin doesn't have this in her documents yet. She says she isn't certain and offers a specialist. She will not invent an age — and in a retirement plan, inventing an age is how someone gets a tax penalty. Then say: "that question is now in her queue automatically, and that's how we decide what to write next."
What this shows

The most valuable thing she does is know what she doesn't know. Every question she can't answer becomes the list of what to teach her next — ranked by how often real members ask it.
