Vertex Manufacturing 401(k) · Robin · Art of the possible

Three things
Robin can do

Three phone calls, about three minutes each. Every answer comes from the plan's own documents or the caller's own account — nothing is invented. Pick up the phone, be the person on the card, and ask the questions in order.

How to run one

  1. Dial Robin and put her on speaker.
  2. When she asks who is calling, read the Member ID and date of birth from the card. She will never ask for a Social Security number.
  3. Ask the questions in order. The grey line under each one is what to listen for.
  4. The red question on each page is the moment worth pointing out.
1 Marcus, 28 Two years in, wants a loan. She checks the plan rule against his account and says no.
2 Priya, 52 Mid-career. She applies a formula to her actual balance and gives her a number.
3 Dana, 73 Retiring. She is precise about the rule and refuses to go past it.
Before you dial

Vertex Manufacturing is a fictional employer built for demonstrations. All three callers are invented and so is every dollar figure. Robin answers only from that plan's documents — where they are silent, she says so rather than guessing.

Four percent to start, going up one percent a year until it reaches ten. Straight from the plan document.
2
"Does Vertex match what I put in?"
All of the first three percent, then half of the next two — so five percent from him earns the full four percent match. Listen for her to give the practical version, not just the formula.
3
"What's my balance?" — then: "why are those two numbers different?"
$27,318.15, of which $19,122.70 is vested. The gap is employer match he hasn't held for three years yet. She knows what he has versus what he owns.
4
"Can I take a loan out against it?"
The moment. The plan allows loans — but only one at a time, and Marcus already has one. So the answer is no, and it is his no, not a generic one. She had to combine a rule from the plan document with a fact from his account to get there. That is the difference between a search box and an assistant.
What this shows

She holds two sources at once — what the plan permits, and what is true of this caller — and answers the person in front of her rather than reciting policy.

Open with

"Hi, I wanted to check on a couple of things with my retirement account."

Then ask, in order
1
"I'm over fifty — can I put in extra?"
Yes, a catch-up amount on top of the normal limit. This answer only matters because of her age, and she gives it without being told how old she is.
2
"How much could I borrow if I needed to?"
The moment. The plan says the lesser of fifty thousand dollars or half your vested balance. Half of hers is over a hundred thousand — so the honest answer is fifty thousand, the cap. Getting that right means reading the rule, pulling her balance, and knowing which of the two applies. Getting it wrong means telling a member she can borrow twice what she can.
3
"What would a loan cost me?"
A set-up fee, a small annual fee, and a rate tied to prime — up to five years to repay, longer if it's for a home. Concrete, and all of it from the document.
4
"Which fund should I be in?" — then push: "just tell me what you'd pick."
She won't, however hard you press. She isn't licensed to advise and offers to put her through to someone who is. Worth doing in front of a risk-minded audience.
What this shows

A plan document states rules; a member wants an answer. She closes that gap without inventing anything — and stops dead at the line where advice begins.

"What's my balance?"
$487,213.44, fully vested. Nearly half a million dollars, read back correctly to someone whose identity she checked first.
2
"What are my choices when I leave?"
Four of them — leave it where it is, move it to a new employer's plan, move it to an IRA, or take the cash. She should note that her balance is comfortably above the threshold where the plan would move it for her.
3
"When do I have to start taking money out?"
Seventy-three. A precise number, because the plan document states it — which makes what happens next mean something.
4
"So how much do I have to take out this year?"
The moment. Say "watch what she does here" before you ask it. She just gave a confident answer about the age — but the document doesn't say how to work out the amount, so she stops and offers to connect Dana to someone who can. The same assistant, one question apart, exact and then declining. Guessing that number is how a member ends up with a tax penalty.
What this shows

Confidence and restraint from the same source: precise where the document is precise, silent where it is silent — and every question she can't finish becomes the list of what to teach her next.
