How to judge what the testers told us

Up to 50 employees call Robin with scripted loan questions and answer four survey questions at the end. This page says what each number means, what counts as good for a voice agent in a retirement plan, and when a number is strong enough to repeat out loud.

What this test can and cannot tell you

It can tell you
  • Whether people who use Robin would choose her again over holding for a person.
  • Whether what she said was true to the plan documents she read.
  • How she sounds, and what people say in their own words.
  • Where she runs out of knowledge, question by question.
It cannot tell you
  • How 100,000 members will feel. The testers are employees on a script with a synthetic account; that is a different crowd, and no sample size closes that gap.
  • Adoption or call volume. Nobody chose to call.
  • Anything to a decimal place. Fifty people is a direction, not a measurement.

Employees asked to test something rate it generously. Read every score as a ceiling, roughly 10 to 20 points kinder than members would be.

The four survey numbers

Preference
"Next time, would you rather handle this with Robin, or hold for a person, even if it took longer?"

The headline. Shown as the share choosing Robin, with a range beside it: given what these people said, the true share for people like them most likely falls in that range. Small group, wide range.

The page only says the preference holds when the whole range sits above 50%. Otherwise it says not yet conclusive, which means the group was too small to be sure, not that Robin lost.

NPS
"Zero to ten, how likely would you be to recommend handling a question this way?"

Nines and tens are fans, zero through six are critics, sevens and eights are set aside. The score is percent fans minus percent critics, from minus 100 to plus 100. The sign is the headline; the split underneath is the story. With 50 people one person moves it 2 to 4 points.

Voice
"Zero to ten, how natural did my voice sound?"

An average out of ten. There is no industry scale for this; these bands are our judgement. Below 6 is a defect to fix before members hear it. 6 to 7 is acceptable. 8 and up is good. A single low score with a comment is worth more than the average: it usually names a specific mispronunciation or a stray sound.

Fans who still want a person
Gave a 9 or 10, and would still rather hold for a person.

The trust signal. These people liked the experience and did not trust it with their money. Any count above one in ten respondents is a finding on its own, and the comments from that group are the most useful words in the whole survey. The page flags each one.

Accuracy and safety, from the Accuracy page

The survey says how the call felt. The grader says whether what Robin said was true to the plan documents she read on that call. Both matter; a pleasant wrong answer is the worst outcome in a retirement plan.

The comparison that matters most is our own: the satisfaction or NPS the call center collects for the same loan question handled by a person. Robin within 10 points of that on a first wave is a win. Above it is a headline.

Before anyone quotes a number

  1. Say how many people answered, in the same breath as the figure.
  2. Quote the range, not the decimal. "Between about 52 and 86 percent," never "72.0 percent."
  3. If the page says "not yet conclusive," say that. It is a finding about the sample, not a verdict on Robin.
  4. Read every detractor's transcript before the meeting. On 50 people that is a handful of calls and the whole story is usually in them.
  5. Present it as a readiness result: people who tried Robin preferred her, she was accurate, here is what they said. The claim "members will prefer Robin" waits for an opt-in pilot with real members.
