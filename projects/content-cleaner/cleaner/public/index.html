How this works

This tool turns a plan document into answer cards — a question a member asks, and the answer Robin gives on the call.

What a card is — four parts, always

Every card is one member question and nothing else. It has the same four parts, in the same order, so a hundred cards stay readable instead of becoming a pile of documents:

  • Question — what a member actually asks, in their words. "Can I take a loan from my 401(k)?", not "Loan Provisions, Section 4.2". This is also how Robin finds the right card when someone asks.
  • Answer — what Robin says. The direct answer first, then the detail. Nothing else lives here.
  • Qualifiers — the conditions that change the answer: "if you're still employed…", "if you already have a loan out…". Spoken, because the answer is wrong without them. Most cards have none, and that's fine — an unconditional fact has no conditions.
  • Notes — for Robin, not the caller. What your document does not settle, so she offers a specialist instead of guessing: "the document doesn't state the repayment term." Notes describe the document, never the plan.

Separating them isn't tidiness. An answer with every exception jammed into it is unlistenable. And a note about what your document leaves out is unverifiable by definition — mixing it into the answer is what used to make honest cards fail the fact-check.

What it does

  1. Reads the document. The cleaner reads what you upload to build an understanding of the context. If the file is a scan — a picture of words rather than words — it says so, because there's nothing to read until it's converted.
  2. Builds the answer cards. One card per member question, in the four parts above.
  3. Waits for you. You are the final check: review each card, fix anything, then publish. Once published, they are live on calls.

Quality assurance

What the checks actually catch:

  • It can't invent a number. If your document says the loan fee is $100, the card says $100. If your document never states the repayment term, that goes in Notes rather than being supplied from general knowledge.
  • A second model fact-checks every card, claim by claim. It pulls out each figure, deadline and rule the card states and finds the exact sentence in your document that backs it up — or reports that there isn't one. You can open any card and read the quotes it matched.
  • Every quote is then checked against your document by the tool itself. This cuts both ways. A quote that isn't really in your file proves nothing, so it stops counting as proof. And a fact the checker couldn't find, which your document plainly contains, is listed as "worth confirming" instead of being scored as invented — because "I didn't find it" is not "it isn't there".
  • A card containing a member's name, Social Security number or account number cannot be saved. It is blocked, not flagged.
  • Nothing publishes on its own — a person presses publish — and any card can be corrected or switched off after it is live.

What the score means

Every card starts at 5.0 and loses points for specific, named evidence. There is no model opinion in the number: a model can be generous with a score, it can't be generous with a quote that doesn't exist. The card shows its own subtraction — "starts at 5.0, then −1.0 a figure your document doesn't state" — so you can disagree with it.

  • Contradicts your document — the worst thing a card can do. −1.5 each.
  • States a figure or rule your document doesn't — −1.0 each. Wording drift with no actionable specifics costs −0.5.
  • Leaves out something your document says — −0.5 each.
  • Doesn't answer its own question — −2.0. Style problems (hard to say aloud, jargon question, padding, a gap left unnoted) are −0.5 each.
  • Every one of these is capped. A long, detailed card makes more claims than a short one, so uncapped per-claim penalties punished depth rather than error — which is exactly how a run ends up looking like all 2s. A card's score now reflects the worst thing in it, not the length of the list.
  • "Worth confirming" costs nothing. Those are the places the fact-checker and your document disagreed about whether something is there. That's a question for you, not a defect.

Anything at 4.5 or above with nothing on its fix list is ready to send. Below that, the card tells you what to change.

How we know it's working

After each call we check what the member asked and whether Robin answered it. That produces two things: a score — how many questions she handled without passing the caller to someone else — and a ranked list of what she couldn't answer.

For example: 47 callers asked how to repay a loan, and Robin had nothing on it. That stays at the top of the list until a card covers it. The list is what decides which document you clean next.

The standard we write to

The cards follow KCS (Knowledge-Centered Service), the support-content method published by the Consortium for Service Innovation. Three of its rules shape the four-part card above:

  • Keep it short. Answer the question, offer more, stop. Robin is speaking, not printing.
  • Write every card the same way, so a hundred cards stay usable instead of becoming a pile of documents.
  • Write for what is actually asked. The ranked list above decides which cards get built next, rather than a guess about what members might want.

Because Robin speaks rather than displays, we add two rules of our own: a card has to make sense heard once, out loud, with no screen — so no tables, no "see the section above", no "click here". And no member's personal information, ever.

What it doesn't do

It doesn't set policy, interpret unclear plan language, or judge legal accuracy. It drafts from the document you gave it and waits for your approval.
